\section{Topology}
\label{sec:topology}

Topology is the study of the poetries of spaces that are preserved under continuous deformations. It provides a framework for understanding the properties of spaces that are invariant under homeomorphisms, which are continuous bijections with continuous inverses. In this section, we will explore some fundamental concepts in topology that are essential for our study of geometric inverse problems. We will begin by discussing the notions of closed and open sets, as well as limit points. Then, we will introduce the concept of topological spaces and continuity. Finally, we will delve into more advanced topics such as compactness, connectivity, homeomorphisms, and homotopy.

\subsection{Closed and Open Sets and Limit Points} One of the most fundamental concepts in topology is that of open and closed sets. These provide the necessary language to describe the ``inside'' and the ``edge'' of a geometric object. Before defining these, we must introduce the notion of a limit point.

\begin{definition}
  A point $x$ is a \emph{limit point} of a set $A$ if every neighborhood of $x$ contains at least one point of $A$ different from $x$ itself. In other words, for every $\epsilon > 0$, the open ball $B(x, \epsilon) = \{y \in X : d(x,y) < \epsilon\}$ contains a point $a \in A$ such that $a \neq x$.
\end{definition}

This definition captures the idea of points being ``arbitrarily close'' to a set. Note that a limit point of $A$ does not necessarily have to be an element of $A$.

\begin{definition}
  An \emph{open set} $O$ is a set such that for every point $x \in O$, there exists an $\epsilon > 0$ such that the open ball $B(x, \epsilon) \subseteq O$. Intuitively, an open set does not contain its own ``edge.'' A \emph{closed set} $C$ is a set such that its complement $X \setminus C$ is open, where $X$ is the ambient space.
\end{definition}

A vital characterization of closed sets is that they \emph{contain all their limit points}. In the study of manifolds with boundary, we often deal with the \emph{closure} of a set.

\begin{definition}
  The \emph{closure} of a set $A$, denoted $\overline{A}$, is the union of $A$ and the set of all its limit points. The \emph{interior} of $A$, denoted $\text{int}(A)$, is the union of all open sets contained in $A$. The \emph{boundary} of $A$ is then defined as:
  \[%
    \partial A = \overline{A} \setminus \text{int}(A)
  .\]%
\end{definition}

In medical imaging, we typically define the ``patient'' as a closed set $M$. The interior $\text{int}(M)$ represents the biological tissue we wish to reconstruct, while the boundary $\partial M$ is the surface where we place our ultrasound transducers.

\begin{example}[Open and Closed Sets in $\R$]
  In the real line $\R$, the interval $(0, 1)$ is an open set. The points $0$ and $1$ are limit points of $(0, 1)$, but they are not contained in the set.

  Conversely, the interval $[0, 1]$ is a closed set. It contains all of its limit points, including the endpoints $0$ and $1$. The interior of this set is $(0, 1)$, and its boundary consists of the discrete points $\{0, 1\}$. In higher dimensions, such as a ball $B^n \subset \R^n$, the boundary $\partial B^n$ becomes the sphere $\S^{n-1}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The Discrete and Trivial Topologies]
  To see that ``openness'' depends on the chosen topology $\tau$, consider a set $X$. In the \emph{discrete topology}, every subset is open. In the \emph{trivial topology}, only $\emptyset$ and $X$ are open. In geometric inverse problems, we almost exclusively utilize the \emph{standard topology} induced by the Riemannian metric $g$, where open sets are generated by open balls $B_g(x, \epsilon)$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Topological Spaces and Continuity} This portion is dedicated to the formalization of topological spaces. While analysis typically studies the properties of functions and spaces preserved under limits within a metric framework, topology generalizes these notions. It defines a structure that allows us to discuss continuity and convergence without strictly requiring a distance function (metric). This is achieved through the concept of a topological space.

\begin{definition}
  A \emph{topological space} $(X, \tau)$ consists of a set $X$ and a collection $\tau$ of subsets of $X$, called \emph{open sets}, such that:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item The empty set $\emptyset$ and the entire set $X$ are in $\tau$.
    \item The union of any arbitrary collection of sets in $\tau$ is also in $\tau$.
    \item The intersection of any \emph{finite} collection of sets in $\tau$ is also in $\tau$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The collection $\tau$ is called a \emph{topology} on $X$. This structure provides the most primitive framework necessary to define ``closeness'' and ``limits.'' In our study of medical imaging, we primarily deal with the \emph{metric topology}.

\begin{example}[The Metric Topology]
  Given a metric space $(M, g)$, we can define a topology $\tau_g$ where a set $U \subseteq M$ is open if and only if for every $x \in U$, there exists an $\epsilon > 0$ such that the open ball $B_g(x, \epsilon) \subseteq U$. This is the standard topology used in Riemannian geometry. In ultrasound tomography, the metric $g$ varies based on tissue density, but the underlying topology of the human body remains equivalent to a subset of $\R^n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The Subspace Topology]
  Since our problem focuses on a manifold $M$ with a boundary $\partial M$, we must define how the boundary inherits its topological structure. If $(X, \tau)$ is a topological space and $Y \subset X$, the \emph{subspace topology} on $Y$ is defined as:
  \[%
    \tau_Y = { U \cap Y : U \in \tau }
  .\]%
  This is how we formally treat the ``skin'' of the patient ($\partial M$) as a topological space in its own right, inherited from the three-dimensional ``body'' ($M$).
\end{example}

By abstracting these properties, we can ensure that when we ``deform'' a manifold smoothly (as we do in the boundary rigidity problem), the fundamental relationship between points--their ``connectedness''--remains intact. This leads us directly to the topological definition of a continuous map, which serves as the foundation for the homeomorphisms and diffeomorphisms that characterize geometric equivalence.

\subsection{Compactness and the Heine-Borel Theorem} One of the most fundamental concepts in topology is that of compactness. It generalizes the idea of a set being ``closed and bounded'' to abstract topological spaces.

\begin{definition}
  A topological space $(X, \tau)$ is \emph{compact} if every open cover of $X$ has a finite subcover. In other words, if $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ is a collection of open sets such that $X \subseteq \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} U_\alpha$, then there exists a finite subset $F \subseteq A$ such that $X \subseteq \bigcup_{\alpha \in F} U_\alpha$.
\end{definition}

Intuitively, compactness suggests that the space is not ``too large'' and does not have any ``missing points.'' In the context of Euclidean spaces, which serve as the ambient space for our medical models, compactness is simplified by the following landmark result:

\begin{theorem}[Heine-Borel]
  A subset $K \subset \R^n$ is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded.
\end{theorem}

\begin{note}
  We won't be proving the Heine-Borel theorem in this paper, as it doesn't directly pertain to the boundary rigidity problem. However, it is crucial to understand that this theorem provides a powerful characterization of compact sets in Euclidean space, which is the setting for our manifold $M$. The requirement that $M$ be compact ensures that we can apply various analytical tools and guarantees the existence of geodesics between points, which are essential for reconstructing the internal structure from boundary measurements.
\end{note}

In our study of the boundary rigidity problem, we assume the manifold $M$ (the patient's body) is a compact manifold with boundary. This assumption is mathematically non-negotiable for several reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Existence of Extremals:} The Extreme Value Theorem states that a continuous function on a compact set must attain its maximum and minimum. This ensures that for any two points in our tissue, there actually exists a path of minimum time (a geodesic).
  \item \textbf{Finiteness of Signal Travel:} Compactness, specifically the ``boundedness'' part of the Heine-Borel theorem, guarantees that every geodesic starting at the boundary must eventually exit the manifold. This is a prerequisite for the \emph{non-trapping condition} discussed in Section 2.
  \item \textbf{Global-to-Local Stability:} Compactness allows us to take local properties (like the injectivity of the exponential map near a point) and extend them to global properties across the entire organ.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}[Compact vs. Non-Compact Sets]
  The closed interval $[0, 1] \subset \R$ is compact. Any sequence of ultrasound arrival times measured within this range will have a convergent subsequence. However, the open interval $(0, 1)$ is \emph{not} compact; a sequence of points could ``leak'' out toward the edge at $0$, never reaching a limit point within the set. In medical imaging, we require the ``skin'' to be part of our set (making it closed) and the patient to be of finite size (making it bounded) to ensure a well-posed inverse problem.
\end{example}

\subsection{Connectivity and Simple Connectivity} Connectivity and simple connectivity describe the ``oneness'' of a space and the absence of fundamental holes. These concepts are vital for ensuring that our measurements at the boundary can reach every part of the interior without being obstructed by topological barriers.

\begin{definition}
  A topological space $X$ is \emph{connected} if it cannot be partitioned into two disjoint, non-empty open sets. A stronger and more intuitive version for manifolds is \emph{path-connectivity}: $X$ is path-connected if for any two points $x, y \in X$, there exists a continuous map $\gamma: [0, 1] \to X$ such that $\gamma(0) = x$ and $\gamma(1) = y$.
\end{definition}

In the context of medical imaging, we assume the manifold $M$ is connected. If $M$ were disconnected (consisting of two ``islands'' of tissue), a signal from a transducer on one component would never reach the other, making it impossible to reconstruct the full interior from a single boundary data set.

\begin{definition}
  A space $X$ is \emph{simply connected} if it is path-connected and every continuous loop in $X$ can be continuously contracted to a single point.
\end{definition}

Technically, a space is simply connected if its \emph{fundamental group} $\pi_1(X)$ is trivial. This property is crucial for the boundary rigidity problem for the following reasons:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Avoidance of Topological Trapping:} On a non-simply connected manifold, such as a torus $\mathbb{T}^2$, there exist geodesics that ``wind'' around the hole and never return to the boundary. Simple connectivity ensures that the ``topology'' of the body doesn't provide a place for sound waves to hide.
  \item \textbf{Uniqueness of Geodesics:} In a simply connected manifold with non-positive curvature, any two points are connected by a \emph{unique} geodesic. This uniqueness is a cornerstone of simple manifolds, which we will define in Chapter 2, ensuring that our ``scans'' don't produce ghost images due to multiple paths between the same source and receiver.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[The Disk vs. The Annulus]
  The unit disk $D^2 = \{(x, y) : x^2 + y^2 \leq 1\}$ is simply connected; any loop of thread on the disk can be pulled tight to a point. However, the annulus $A = \{(x, y) : 0.5 \leq x^2 + y^2 \leq 1\}$ is connected but \emph{not} simply connected. A loop that goes around the center hole cannot be contracted. In ultrasound tomography, an annulus-shaped domain (like a cross-section of a limb with a bone ``hole'' modeled as a boundary) requires significantly more complex math to ensure that the rays probe the tissue correctly without getting caught in orbits around the central void.
\end{example}

\subsection{Homeomorphisms and Diffeomorphisms} While connectivity and compactness describe the intrinsic nature of a single space, homeomorphisms and diffeomorphisms allow us to compare two different spaces to determine if they are ``equivalent'' in a topological or geometric sense.

\begin{definition}
  A \emph{homeomorphism} is a bijection $f: X \to Y$ between two topological spaces such that both $f$ and its inverse $f^{-1}$ are continuous. If such a map exists, $X$ and $Y$ are said to be \emph{homeomorphic}, and they are indistinguishable from a purely topological perspective.
\end{definition}

In topology, a homeomorphism can stretch, twist, or bend a space, but it cannot tear it or glue parts together. For instance, a sphere and a cube are homeomorphic. However, for the study of Riemannian manifolds and medical imaging, continuity is not enough. We require the deformation to be smooth so that we can consistently define velocities and curvatures.

\begin{definition}
  A \emph{diffeomorphism} is a homeomorphism $f: M \to N$ between two smooth manifolds such that both $f$ and its inverse $f^{-1}$ are infinitely differentiable ($C^\infty$).
\end{definition}

In our notation established in Section~\ref{sec:notation}, a diffeomorphism ensures that the Jacobian matrix $J^\mu_\nu = \pd{x^\nu} f^\mu$ is well-defined and invertible at every point. This is the ``gold standard'' of equivalence in geometric inverse problems.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Geometric Rigidity:} The boundary rigidity problem asks whether the boundary distance function $d_g$ uniquely determines the metric $g$ \emph{up to a diffeomorphism}. This means that if two metrics $g$ and $\hat{g}$ produce the same travel times on the boundary, there must exist a diffeomorphism $\psi: M \to M$ that fixes the boundary such that $\hat{g} = \psi^* g$.
  \item \textbf{Clinical Interpretation:} In medical imaging, a diffeomorphism represents a smooth re-parametrization of the internal tissue. If we reconstruct a tumor, we want to ensure it is in the correct ``geometric'' location. If our reconstruction is only accurate up to a diffeomorphism, it means we have the right ``shape'' and ``structure,'' but the coordinate grid might be slightly warped.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[The Deformation of Tissue]
  Imagine a block of tissue $M$ being compressed by an ultrasound probe. The compression is a diffeomorphism: the tissue is deformed smoothly, and points that were close together remain close together. The ``topological'' properties (connectivity, compactness) remain unchanged, but the ``geometric'' properties (the distance between two internal points) are altered. The goal of boundary rigidity is to ``see through'' these possible deformations to identify the true underlying metric.
\end{example}

\subsection{The Concept of Homotopy} While homeomorphisms allow us to deform entire spaces, \emph{homotopy} provides a way to continuously deform functions or paths within a space. This concept is essential for understanding how a ``fan'' of geodesics (ultrasound rays) covers the interior of a manifold.

\begin{definition}
  Two continuous maps $f, g: X \to Y$ are said to be \emph{homotopic} if there exists a continuous map $H: X \times [0, 1] \to Y$, called a \emph{homotopy}, such that for all $x \in X$:
  \[%
    H(x, 0) = f(x) \aand H(x, 1) = g(x)
  .\]%
\end{definition}

If we view $t \in [0, 1]$ as a time parameter, the homotopy $H$ describes a movie where the map $f$ smoothly transforms into $g$. In the study of geometric inverse problems, we are specifically interested in \emph{path homotopy}.

\begin{definition}
  Two paths $\gamma_0, \gamma_1 : [0, 1] \to M$ with the same endpoints $p$ and $q$ are homotopic relative to their endpoints if there exists a homotopy $H$ such that every intermediate path $H(\cdot, t)$ also starts at $p$ and ends at $q$.
\end{definition}

In the context of the boundary rigidity problem, homotopy plays a decisive role in the following areas:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scanning Continuity:} As a clinician moves an ultrasound probe along the boundary $\partial M$, the resulting geodesics vary continuously. Homotopy ensures that the interior is sampled by a continuous ``sheet'' of rays, leaving no ``mathematical holes'' in the data.
  \item \textbf{Simply Connected Domains:} In Section 1.5, we defined a simply connected space as one where every loop is homotopic to a constant map (a point). For simple manifolds, this property guarantees that the ``phase space'' of geodesics is topologically uncomplicated, which is a requirement for the injectivity of the X-ray transform.
  \item \textbf{Conjugate Points:} One of the key results in Riemannian geometry is that if two paths between $p$ and $q$ are homotopic but not equivalent, the manifold must possess conjugate points or non-trivial topology. By requiring our manifold to be ``simple,'' we essentially use homotopy to prove that there is only \emph{one} way for a signal to travel between any two points.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[The ``Fan'' of Rays]
  Consider a point source at $x \in \partial M$ emitting a fan of geodesics $\gamma_\theta(t)$ into the body, where $\theta$ is the initial angle. The map $H(\theta, t) = \gamma_\theta(t)$ is a homotopy. Because the tissue is a smooth manifold, this homotopy ensures that if we detect a tumor with ray $\gamma_{\theta_1}$, we will also see a similar (slightly shifted) signal in the neighboring ray $\gamma_{\theta_1 + \epsilon}$. This redundancy is exactly what allows reconstruction algorithms to ``smooth out'' noise and produce clear medical images.
\end{example}
